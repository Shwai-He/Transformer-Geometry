\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textit{This geometric decomposition suggests practical uses beyond the intervention
analysis, especially for diagnosing compression error and interpreting
training-time allocation.}

\paragraph{Compression diagnostics.}
Approximation error should not be viewed only as an overall residual magnitude.
In our results, perpendicular error is the more fragile component: perturbations
that change the update direction are more damaging than direction-preserving
errors. However, the near-identity between total- and perpendicular-error
rankings is matched by a norm-controlled isotropic null, so perpendicular error
should not be presented as independent predictive information beyond total
magnitude. Its appropriate role here is a component-resolved diagnostic whose
functional meaning is calibrated by the controlled edits.

\paragraph{Practical screening workflow.}
For a proposed edit or compression rule, we recommend four steps: (1) specify
the exact intervention location and reference direction; (2) use matched
directional ablations to determine which component is sensitive at that
location; (3) compare the candidate's orientation with total-error magnitude
and an appropriate matched null; and (4) use geometry as a screening diagnostic
before validating the selected configuration on the downstream task. The
layer/head results add an important guardrail: prevalence alone is not a
universal ranking rule because sensitivity varies with depth and joint head
effects are non-additive.

\paragraph{Training-time allocation.}
Inference-time edits suggest that value-space parallel components can be
suppressed with comparatively little behavioral change, whereas perpendicular
changes are more fragile. The training runs test whether suppressing this route
changes how optimization allocates learning pressure across the remaining
updates. The full-scale single V-Para runs improve average downstream score,
whereas an early matched three-seed study improves validation loss but not the
six-task downstream average. We therefore treat the training result as evidence
that the constraint changes optimization, not as a replicated downstream-gain
claim or convergence theorem. Checkpoint-level geometry further supports
operation-dependent co-adaptation in the tested setting.
