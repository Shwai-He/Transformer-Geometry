\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Intervention implementation.}
All edits are forward-pass-only and leave model weights unchanged. We apply the
component-scaling interventions from \S\ref{sec:closed-form-control} either in
value space before \(W_O\) or in residual space after branch outputs are
formed. The retained-scale value-space benchmarks edit every decoder layer
except the first; residual-space controls use the layer ranges specified with
their experiment. Parallel removal uses \(\spara=0,\sperp=1\); scaling sweeps
vary one scale while fixing the other at 1. \textit{Diag. Rem.} is a hard
attention-diagonal baseline that sets \(A_{tt}=0\) and renormalizes the row.
Unless marked \textit{Raw V}, value-space results use the exclude-self
definition in Eq.~\ref{eq:exclude-self-scaling}: they hold
$A_{tt}\mathbf{v}_t$ fixed, decompose only the non-self aggregate, and do not
renormalize attention.

\paragraph{Pretraining setup and calibration.}
For pretraining, we train GPT-style models from 194M to 528M parameters from
scratch on standard next-token prediction, with baseline and parallel-removal
variants. We also run larger 1.4B and 2.7B models for downstream evaluation
(Table~\ref{tab:pretraining-summary}). Full hyperparameters are in
Appendix~\ref{app:training-configs}. Diagnostic sample counts and corpora are
reported with each result; C4-based analyses use fixed subsets and average
per-token quantities within the corresponding subset.

\subsection{Component Editing Benchmarks}
\label{sec:zeroshot}

Sections~\ref{sec:residual-level} and~\ref{sec:value-level} define the same
parallel/perpendicular geometry in residual and value spaces. This subsection
tests the functional role of these components by measuring how sensitive model
behavior is to direct parallel and perpendicular edits at inference time.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figs/para_ablation_corrected/ppl_component_scaling_corrected.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Perplexity change under component scaling on Qwen3-1.7B.}
           We vary the parallel scale $\spara$ (left) or perpendicular scale
           $\sperp$ (right), comparing exclude-self value-space manipulation
           with residual-space attention and MLP variants. Every curve uses
           the full 62-document WikiText evaluator and edits all decoder layers
           except the first; all three parallel curves use the same eleven
           retained scales from $-1$ to $3$. Each point reports
           $\Delta\mathrm{PPL}=\mathrm{PPL}_{\mathrm{edited}}-
           \mathrm{PPL}_{\mathrm{baseline}}$ relative to its corresponding
           scale-1 hook-on no-op. Both panels use a symmetric-log vertical
           axis, and scale 1 is the matched no-op for each curve. Exclude-self
           is defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:exclude-self-scaling}; it preserves the
           direct self message and edits only the non-self aggregate.}
  \label{fig:residual-component-editing}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Component-scaling results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:residual-component-editing} varies \(\spara\) (left) and
\(\sperp\) (right) independently for three intervention sites. On the common
parallel grid, exclude-self value-space scaling is comparatively stable within
the unit interval: its largest absolute PPL change is 1.32, and full removal
changes PPL by only 0.46, whereas fully removing either residual-space parallel
component increases PPL by about 21--22. This local robustness does not extend
arbitrarily: the value-space delta reaches 18.13 at scale $-0.5$, 188.77 at
scale $-1$, and 67.44 at scale $3$. Outside the unit interval, Residual(Attn)
is still far more sensitive to sign reversal, while Residual(MLP) is far more
sensitive to strong positive amplification. Suppressing the perpendicular
component is substantially more disruptive, especially for Residual(MLP),
although a narrow neighborhood around the no-op can contain small improvements.
Thus, the sweep supports both a directional asymmetry and a coordinate-system
distinction: robustness is not inherent to parallelness alone, but depends on
which object is edited and which parts of the update are preserved.

Appendix~\ref{app:method} formalizes this distinction. \textit{The scaling
sweep uses component editing as a functional probe. We next turn the probe into
a sharper removal test of whether value-space parallel removal preserves
performance on standard downstream and long-context evaluations.}

\subsection{Value-Space vs.\ Residual-Space Editing}
\label{sec:value-vs-residual-exp}

We compare three approaches: (1) \textit{value-space parallel removal}, (2)
\textit{hard diagonal masking}, and (3) \textit{residual-space variants}.

\paragraph{General benchmark comparison.}
Table~\ref{tab:general-zeroshot} reports the retained-scale
comparison on the same seven non-generative tasks. Raw full value-space
parallel removal is not lossless: it reduces the mean by 8.2--10.0 points.
Preserving the direct self contribution and editing only the non-self aggregate
is substantially more robust, especially on the 30B-A3B model, and retaining
half of the parallel component is near baseline. Hard diagonal masking remains
far more disruptive because it deletes the self edge and renormalizes the
attention row. Thus the robust target is not an arbitrary parallel component;
it depends on preserving direct self routing and the remaining perpendicular
context contribution.

Table~\ref{tab:longcontext-ruler} shows the short-context comparison.
At 4k--12k, retaining half of the value-space parallel component is
comparatively stable, especially when the explicit self term is preserved;
full removal is damaging even under the exclude-self definition. The complete
4k--128k family-macro comparison in
Appendix~\ref{tab:appendix-ruler-space-comparison} reveals a clear length
boundary: at 128k, exclude-self half retention scores 44.9 against its matched
55.2 baseline, while raw half retention scores 40.6. Residual-space variants
are already far below their matched baseline at 4k and remain degraded as
context grows, whether the edit acts on attention, MLP, or both branches.
Thus value-space half retention is the less disruptive intervention, but its
robustness does not extend unchanged to the longest contexts.
\paragraph{Attention diagonal and parallel geometry.}
\label{sec:diagonal-validation}
All three attention-side interventions can be rewritten as effective diagonal
edits, but they induce different diagonal coefficients. The removed-case row in
Table~\ref{tab:effective-diagonal-derivation} shows the key distinction:
value-based removal uses value-space similarities
\(\mathbf{v}_s^\top\mathbf{v}_t/\|\mathbf{v}_t\|^2\), the residual-space
analogue uses post-\(W_O\) similarities against the residual reference
direction, and hard diagonal removal sets \(\widetilde A_{tt}=0\) before
renormalizing attention over non-self tokens. This contrast explains why
parallel-edit robustness is direction-sensitive: parallel removal stays close
to baseline only when the edit is aligned with the self-value-parallel
direction.

  \centering

  \caption{\textbf{Diagonal-edit attention maps on Qwen3-4B.}
  At layer 19, the left panel shows raw softmax attention for head 6. The
  middle panel shows the layer-level shared-diagonal coefficient equivalent to
  residual-space attention parallel removal after $W_O$; it is not assigned
  to head 6 because $W_O$ mixes heads. The right panel shows the head-6
  pre-$W_O$ value-space coefficient. The two delta panels share the same
  $[-1,1]$ color scale.}
  \label{fig:attn-maps}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:attn-maps} compares the effective diagonal changes induced by
two parallel edits on Qwen3-4B. The recomputed residual-space coefficients are
not uniformly tiny: they span negative and positive values and reach order-one
magnitude. In contrast, the selected head's value-space coefficients are all
negative. The difference follows from their reference directions and from the
fact that residual editing occurs after cross-head mixing. This view clarifies
why the intervention must be reference-aware. Hard diagonal masking is the
separate control in Table~\ref{tab:general-zeroshot}: it sets the self entry to
zero and renormalizes over non-self tokens, rather than applying either
effective parallel-edit coefficient shown here.

The all-layer averages also conceal substantial head and depth heterogeneity.
Appendix~\ref{app:head-localization} reports isolated-head sensitivities and
joint all-head controls across four Qwen3 scales.

\subsection{Compression-Induced Error Geometry}
\label{sec:compression-error-geometry}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.97\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/comp_analysis/local_flip_compare-attn_out-perp_over_base_update.pdf}
    \caption{Perpendicular attention-output error.}
  \end{subfigure}

  \vspace{0.25em}
  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.97\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/comp_analysis/local_flip_compare-attn_out-para_over_base_update.pdf}
    \caption{Parallel attention-output error.}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{\textbf{Compression error separates into perpendicular and parallel
  directions.}
  We compare the compressed attention update with the baseline attention update
  across layers. More aggressive compression produces much larger perpendicular
  error, while parallel error is smaller and less consistently ordered.}
  \label{fig:local-flip-attn}
\end{figure}

\textit{The removal experiments show that model behavior is especially
sensitive to direction-changing components of activation updates.} We next ask
how this picture appears when the model itself is modified. Compression changes
the parameters, so its effect is not an explicit activation edit; instead, it
induces an error between the baseline update and the compressed-model update.
We therefore decompose this compression-induced update error into parallel and
perpendicular parts. If perpendicular changes are the more behaviorally
sensitive component, then stronger compressed models should tend to keep this
component small, while parallel error may be less consistently ordered.

Concretely, for the same input at a certain depth (we omit the layer index for
simplicity), let
\(\Delta_{\mathrm{base}}\) denote the baseline update vector and
\(\Delta_{\mathrm{comp}}\) denote the corresponding update after compression.
The local compression error is
\(e=\Delta_{\mathrm{comp}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{base}}\). We then decompose this
error relative to the baseline-update direction,
\(e_{\parallel}=\mathrm{proj}_{\Delta_{\mathrm{base}}}e\), and define
\(e_{\perp}=e-e_{\parallel}\). The y-axis reports the corresponding normalized
component magnitude, averaged over calibration examples.

Figure~\ref{fig:local-flip-attn} measures perpendicular and parallel error across
compression settings on attention outputs. Perpendicular error is ordered with
compression severity, while parallel-error curves from different sparsity
levels interleave. Appendix~\ref{app:compression} also reports the total update
error \(\|e\|/\|\Delta_{\mathrm{base}}\|\), whose layerwise ranking is strongly
aligned with the perpendicular component for attention outputs. A norm-matched
isotropic control shows that high-dimensional concentration substantially
explains this ranking overlap, so we treat it as component-resolved descriptive
consistency rather than evidence that perpendicular error predicts quality
beyond total-error magnitude. The directional view is also related to prior
compression diagnostics based on layer-drop cosine. Matched quality evaluations
in Appendix~\ref{app:compression} show that residual-perpendicular weighting
preserves standard-WANDA performance with small, non-universal differences.
The appendix provides these quality comparisons, null controls, branch-wise
views, derivation, and supporting baseline-update magnitude
plot.

\subsection{Training-Time Allocation}
\label{sec:pretraining}

The inference-time editing results show that parallel and perpendicular updates
play different functional roles, and that the effect of a parallel edit depends
on the space in which it is applied. We therefore test whether suppressing
parallel attention components during training, in residual or value space,
changes how optimization allocates pressure across the remaining attention
update. We apply parallel removal during training and compare the resulting
learning trajectories and downstream scores.
The main text focuses on these parallel-removal training runs, with
Appendix~\ref{app:training-configs} providing the training configurations and
the gated scale diagnostic.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/loss_curves_corrected/pretraining_loss_by_size_restored.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Training-time parallel removal changes validation-loss
  trajectories across model sizes.}
  Curves show the retained single-run trajectories for baseline training,
  residual-space Attn Para-Rem., and value-space V-Para Rem. from 194M to
  528M parameters.}
  \label{fig:pretraining}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:pretraining} shows that suppressing parallel attention updates
changes the optimization trajectory. The separation from baseline appears early
in training and remains visible across model sizes. We additionally repeat
matched fixed-validation evaluations across four model sizes, three seeds, and
four checkpoints. At the final tested checkpoint, V-Para Rem. lowers validation
loss in all 12 architecture--seed pairs; Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:appendix-multiseed-validation} reports the paired results.

Table~\ref{tab:pretraining-summary} reports downstream evaluation for the
larger single pretraining runs. Attn Para-Rem. improves the average score over
baseline, and V-Para Rem. gives the strongest average improvement at both
scales. A separate matched three-seed 0.7B study at 524.288M tokens gives a
validation-loss delta of $-0.1785\pm0.0086$, but its six-task downstream
$\Delta$Avg is $-0.35\pm1.19$ points. Thus, the intervention reproducibly changes
the tested early optimization outcome, while the full-scale downstream gains
remain single-run observations. Beyond
direct removal, we also considered gated scaling of the
parallel component, but the gain is limited; Appendix~\ref{app:training-configs}
reports this diagnostic. We therefore focus on direct parallel removal as the
training-time intervention.
